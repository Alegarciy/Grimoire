## Strong points you can defend

### 1. Merging `Reservation` and `Ticket` into `TicketReservation`

This is the most important design choice, and it is defendable.

**Defense:**
`Reservation` from the Reservation DB and `Ticket` from the Accounting DB refer to nearly the same business event: a seat for a specific projection in a specific room. The accounting side adds commercial information such as `Price` and `InvoiceID`, while the reservation side contributes the reservation structure. Therefore, integrating them into one conceptual entity reduces redundancy and better reflects the real-world domain.

That is a strong GCS justification.

---

### 2. `Projection` as the bridge between movie scheduling and booking

Your model correctly places `Projection` in the center of the cinema event flow:

* `Movie` → `Projection`
* `TicketReservation` → `Projection`

**Defense:**
A movie is not booked directly; what customers reserve or buy is a seat for a specific projection. Therefore, `Projection` is the correct entity connecting the movie domain to the transaction domain.

That is a very good conceptual point.

---

### 3. Keeping `Seat` and `Room` separate

This is also a good choice.

**Defense:**
A seat is a physical object that exists within a room, while a room has its own descriptive characteristic (`ScreenSize`). Since management wants to analyze theatre facilities, keeping `Room` as a distinct entity is important. Likewise, `Seat` must remain explicit because the operational systems identify bookings using seat and room information.

This is strong and consistent with the source schemas.

---

### 4. Keeping `Invoice` and `Customer` on the accounting side

Good decision.

**Defense:**
Customer information is only available through the accounting system, via `Invoice`. The reservation source does not contain customer information. Therefore, the GCS keeps `Invoice` and `Customer` connected to the integrated booking entity rather than forcing a direct relationship unsupported by the source systems.

That is a precise integration argument.

## Specific relationship-by-relationship feedback

## `Movie — Displays — Projection`

Conceptually correct, though if you want to sound more precise in the paper, you might phrase it as:

* a **Projection displays a Movie**
  or
* a **Movie is displayed in Projections**

Your current relationship is acceptable. Just be ready to explain cardinality:

* one movie can be displayed in many projections
* each projection displays one movie

---

## `Projection — Books — TicketReservation`

The existence of the relationship is correct, but the label is the weakest part.

What the model means is:

* one projection can have many ticket reservations
* each ticket reservation is for one projection

So in your defense, do **not** say:

* “projection books ticket reservation”

Instead say:

* **TicketReservation is for Projection**
* or **Projection is associated with many TicketReservations**

The relationship is conceptually right; the label is just less natural.

---

## `TicketReservation — Reserves — Seat`

This is defensible.

**Defense:**
The integrated transaction identifies the seat that is reserved or sold for a given projection. Therefore, associating `TicketReservation` with `Seat` is necessary to preserve the operational meaning of the original systems.

Cardinality you should mention:

* one seat can appear in many ticket reservations over time
* each ticket reservation refers to one seat

That “over time” part is important.

## `Seat — Has — Room`

This is correct in meaning, though the reading should really be:

* a room has many seats
* a seat belongs to one room

So for your oral defense, talk in that direction, even if the diamond says `Has`.

That is a fair defense.

## Key weakness you should acknowledge before someone else points it out

The main thing I would proactively mention in the final paper is this:

**Some relationships are intentionally explicit even when derivable from others.**

For example:

* `TicketReservation → Room`
* while also having
* `TicketReservation → Projection → Room`
  and
* `TicketReservation → Seat → Room`

If you state this openly, it stops being a hidden weakness and becomes a deliberate design choice.

A good sentence for the paper:

> The GCS intentionally keeps some relationships explicit, even where they could be inferred transitively, in order to preserve closeness to the operational sources and improve interpretability of the integrated schema.

That is a solid academic-style defense.

## One issue to be careful with in the write-up: identifier naming

You should be careful with `CustomerID`.

In the original problem statement, the customer entity is:

* `Customer(CPR, FullName, Address, City, TelephoneNumber)`

while the invoice has:

* `Invoice(InvoiceID, Date, CustomerID)`

So in the paper, it would be smart to mention that:

* `CustomerID` is used in the integrated model as the linking customer identifier from the accounting source,
* while `CPR` remains an identifying attribute of the customer entity.

This avoids confusion if someone notices the mismatch.
